How the Kanji drill works

It's a little practice app for learning the 200 kanji of the UK GCSE in Japanese, and the words that go with them. I built it to help with the exam, but it works for anyone learning these characters. It runs in your phone's browser, there's no account to make, and nothing you do leaves your phone.

Not an official app. This isn't made or checked by Pearson or any exam board. The 200 kanji are the ones in Pearson's published list for the Edexcel GCSE (9–1) in Japanese (Appendix 4 of the specification, Issue 5). The meanings, readings and example words were drafted for this app, so if you spot a mistake, please tell me.

What is a kanji?

Kanji are the characters Japanese borrowed from Chinese. Each one stands for an idea: 山 is mountain, 三 is three, 日 is sun or day. Japanese writing mixes kanji with hiragana and katakana, with the kanji carrying the meaning and the kana filling in the grammar.

A kanji isn't always a word on its own, and it usually has more than one way to be read. 三 is san in 三月 (March) but mit- in 三つ (three things). So this app teaches what each kanji means first, and teaches how it's read through real words, never on its own.

Getting going

When you open it you'll see the chart. It's split into the two GCSE tiers, Foundation (76 kanji) and Higher (124 more), and each tier is made of small sets of about 8 to 12 kanji, starting with the numbers. A set stays greyed out until you add it. Tap one and you get two choices:

  • I already know these. You get a quick check, one card per kanji: what does it mean? Anything you get right starts a bit further along, and anything you miss starts from scratch.
  • They're new to me. Each one gets a little intro card first (the kanji, its meaning, how it's read and a few words that use it) before you're tested on it.

Add sets as you get to them. You can add the lot on day one, but you'll get a mountain of reviews if you do.

The questions

  • What does this mean? You see a kanji and pick its meaning.
  • Which kanji is this? You see the meaning and pick the kanji from four that look alike.
  • Write this one. You see the meaning, draw the kanji with your finger and tap Check. The right answer is drawn in red over your writing, one numbered stroke at a time, so you can compare the two. You mark this one yourself, so be honest with yourself. If you miss it, Practise walks you through it: watch, trace, copy, then from memory.
  • How is this read? This is a word, like 三月, and you pick how it's read from four choices. For words that have a voice recording, about half of these are listening questions instead: you hear the word and pick which written word it was. If you're somewhere you can't play sound, tap "Can't use sound right now".

You start with meanings. Picking the kanji from its meaning unlocks once you know what it means, and writing unlocks after that. Words that use a kanji start once you know what the kanji means, and only once every kanji in the word is in a set you've added.

That's the Automatic setting. If you'd rather choose for yourself, go to Settings → What to practise, pick Manual and tick the kinds of question you want. Nothing has to unlock first.

The wrong answers aren't random, either. They're the ones people really do mix up, like 日 and 目, or 人 and 入. Everything's shuffled too, so you can't coast.

After a word question the app shows you the reading and meaning, and when you get one wrong it nudges you to say the word out loud. The app can't hear you, so that part is up to you, but speaking is a quarter of the GCSE, so it's worth doing.

Memory hints

Hints are little stories that help a kanji stick. The app doesn't come with any ready-made ones yet, but you can write your own: tap a set on the chart and then a kanji, or tap Write your own hint under any card, and type whatever helps you remember it, however daft. Your hints show up when that kanji is new, and again when you get it wrong.

Please don't guess. If you're not sure, tap Don't know. A lucky guess tells the app you know something you don't, and it'll leave that card alone for days. "Don't know" brings it back a few cards later and keeps it coming round until it sticks.

How the spaced repetition works

The idea is pretty simple. You remember things best if you go over them just as you're about to forget them. Too soon is a waste of your time, and too late means you've already lost it.

The app uses a scheduling method called FSRS (the same one Anki offers). For every card it keeps an estimate of how well you know it, and books the next review for roughly the point where you'd still have about a 90% chance of getting it right.

In practice that means:

  • Get one wrong and it comes back in about a minute, then ten minutes, and then you build it back up again.
  • Get one right and the gaps get longer: a couple of days, then about ten days, then a month or so.
  • If you answer quickly, it counts as easy and the gap stretches further. If you answer slowly, it counts as hard and the gap grows less.
  • No gap is ever longer than 60 days. Picking from four answers is easier than remembering from nothing, so I didn't want it getting overconfident.

So the ones you always know drift away and only pop up now and then, and most of your time goes on the ones you find tricky.

The buttons on the home screen

  • Start review does everything that's due right now, up to 30 cards at a time, so sessions stay short.
  • Practise weakest gives you extra goes at your shakiest cards.
  • Run through everything gives you one card for every kanji and word you've started, all shuffled. In Manual it's every one in your sets, started or not. It's good for a quick check before a test.

Practising and running through don't mess up your schedule. Getting one right there doesn't push it further away, but getting one wrong does bring it back sooner.

Two or three short sessions a day work much better than one long one. Five minutes on the bus is plenty.

What the colours mean

Each kanji on the chart is coloured by how well you know it across its meaning, recall and writing cards:

  • Learning: still new, or you've just got it wrong.
  • Shaky: getting there, but it'll need checking again soon.
  • Good: you mostly know it.
  • Solid: you know it well enough that it won't need checking for a couple of weeks or more.

Words aren't on the chart. The Progress screen has a Words section instead.

Progress

Tap Progress at the top for a report on the last week or two: how many days you've practised, how accurate you are on each kind of question, which kanji and words you miss most and which pairs you keep mixing up. Share this report sends it as a message, for a teacher or a parent, say.

Where your progress is kept

Everything is saved on your phone, inside the browser. There's no account and nothing gets sent anywhere. That does mean a few things:

  • Everyone using it on their own phone has their own progress.
  • If you change phone or browser, go to Settings → Copy backup on the old one, then paste it into Restore from backup on the new one.
  • Backups only work between Kanji drills. A backup from the Hiragana or Katakana drill is turned away.
  • iPhones can clear data for websites you haven't used in a long while. Adding it to your home screen (see below) mostly stops that, and a backup now and then doesn't hurt.

Putting it on your phone

You can install it so it has its own icon and opens full screen like a normal app. After the first visit it works with no signal at all.

  • iPhone: open it in Safari, tap Share, then Add to Home Screen, and leave Open as Web App switched on. One catch: on an iPhone the home screen app keeps its own progress, separate from Safari. If you've already been practising in Safari, copy a backup first and restore it in the home screen app.
  • Android: open it in Chrome, tap the ⋮ menu, then Install app (or Add to home screen). On Android your progress is shared with Chrome, so nothing needs moving.
  • On a computer: it works in any browser. In Chrome or Edge you can install it too, using the little install icon at the end of the address bar. On a Mac in Safari, use File → Add to Dock.

When there's an update it downloads in the background. Close the app and open it again (sometimes twice) and you'll have it.

Using a keyboard

On a computer you can do the whole thing from the keyboard:

  • 1 to 4 pick an answer, and 0 is Don't know.
  • Enter or Space moves on: Next, Got it, Check, Next step, Show me, or Start review on the home screen.
  • Y and N mark your writing (Got it or Not quite), and C clears the drawing (or, once you've marked it, starts a practice go).
  • P plays the sound again, and Esc closes a pop-up panel.

Sound

If you can't hear anything, check the silent switch on the side of your iPhone first. That catches most people out.

The voice clips were made with Google's text-to-speech, one clip for each example word, and they're being added set by set. Words that don't have a clip yet show up as reading questions only. If you're curious, here's how the voice clips are made. If you'd rather use your phone's own voice, you can switch in Settings.

Feedback and ideas

This app is new and I'm improving it all the time, so I'd love to hear what you think. Spotted a wrong meaning or reading? Got a memory hint that works well? Tap Settings → Send feedback. It opens your email app with a message ready to go, and it can include your own hints if you like.

The first time you meet each kind of card, a little tip pops up to explain it. If you want to see them again, tap Settings → Show tips again. The welcome screens are there too.
